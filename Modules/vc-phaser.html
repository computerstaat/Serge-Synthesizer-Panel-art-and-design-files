How it works

The input runs through a chain of all-pass stages. An all-pass stage leaves the level of every frequency alone but delays its phase, and the amount of delay depends on frequency and on the control voltage. Taps along the chain give outputs shifted by 360°, 720° and 1080°.

On their own the shifted outputs sound almost the same as the input, because the ear hardly hears phase. The effect appears when a shifted signal is mixed back with the original: frequencies that come out of phase cancel, cutting a row of notches into the spectrum. The MIXED output does this inside the module with the 1080° tap, and the MIX knob sets the balance.

PHASE sets the shift by hand. The VC input, through its attenuator, sweeps it from an envelope, LFO or sequencer; the response follows a log curve so sweeps sound even across the range.

Ken Stone's CGS90 puts two small audio mixers on the same PCB. Each has three inputs with level knobs plus one unity-gain x1 input. They are inverting and AC coupled, which makes them good for blending the raw phase outputs with the dry signal (straight or inverted) and for feedback patches: mix the phaser's output with its input before it goes back in. Patch one mixer's output into the other's x1 input to get a single six-input mixer.

Ken Stone uses standard quad op-amps in place of the original RC4136 (LM3403 or TL074), and a 741 or TL071 in the mixers.

The CGS90 board carries the phaser and both mixers, so the 4" Phaser with Mixers panel uses every jack on the board; the 2" Phaser panel leaves the mixers unused.
